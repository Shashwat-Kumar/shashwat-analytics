India's AI rules may test powerful models before launch. The Centre's own note says the data is not ready

MeitY is expected to publish a consultation paper on AI rules as early as December, with a possible 60-day comment window and separate safety requirements for frontier models, NDTV Profit reported on 16 November. A day earlier, a Centre note to the states said that what India's AI effort lacks "is not compute but content".

By Shashwat Kumar · 2026-11-17 · 5 min read · Analysis

Facts are attributed and linked to their sources. The analysis and opinions are the author's own.

What it is

NDTV Profit reported, citing sources, that the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology could release its consultation paper on AI rules as early as next month, possibly with a 60-day window for public comment. The government is weighing separate safety requirements for frontier models, possibly defined by computing power and capabilities. The framework may require developers to carry out safety testing before releasing models to the public, and could include sandbox trials. The report says sectoral regulators such as the RBI, SEBI and TRAI could introduce tailored compliance requirements, and that the government is considering legislation in the Budget Session.

The paper has been coming for some time. Moneycontrol reported on 4 November that minister Ashwini Vaishnaw announced the consultation on 8 October, saying the paper would be released within a month. In an earlier report, NDTV said the framework could examine human approval of sensitive actions by AI agents, restrictions on access to critical systems, monitoring, incident reporting and "mechanisms to stop an agent when its behaviour becomes unsafe".

A day before the NDTV Profit report, The Economic Times reported on a note the Centre sent to states ahead of the sixth National Conference of Chief Secretaries. Despite 38,000 GPUs empanelled under the IndiaAI Mission, it said, "what is missing is not compute but content". Most state datasets are "scheme-specific, vendor-hosted, inconsistently coded and without metadata". Uncertainty about the data protection rules means the default response is "to share nothing at all". And the note warns that without risk assessments or audit trails before AI touches entitlements, citizens without smartphones or Hindi or English literacy risk being excluded altogether.

Why it matters

The two stories describe two different problems. Rules for frontier models are aimed at a handful of developers, most of them abroad. The Centre's note is about the harm closer to home: AI deciding entitlements on badly labelled data, with no audit trail and no way for someone to object. Both matter, but only the second is entirely within India's control.

Rules for frontier labs will make the headlines. An audit trail before AI touches a citizen's entitlement would change more lives.

For banks and other regulated firms, the likely shape is two layers: horizontal rules from MeitY and sectoral requirements from their own regulators. Issue 08 reported that SEBI says its own AI guidelines are coming soon. In my view, firms should not wait for either text. The things every draft so far points to are an inventory of AI systems, evidence of testing before deployment, a way to stop an agent, and a record of who approved what. All four are worth building now, and are much harder to assemble after an incident. One caution: the reported details come from unnamed sources, and the paper itself has not been published.

What I'd watch

  • The consultation paper itself: how it defines a frontier model, and whether deployers as well as developers carry duties.
  • Whether the RBI and SEBI publish sector-specific AI requirements alongside it.
  • Whether the chief secretaries' conference produces commitments on state data standards and audit trails.
