Banks' trade bodies want an off switch for the AI inside the software they buy

AFME, the Loan Market Association and UK Finance published eight recommendations for technology and service providers on 16 November. The most practical: let the bank turn AI features off, ship them separately from unrelated updates and give notice before they arrive.

By Shashwat Kumar · 2026-11-17 · 4 min read · Analysis

Facts are attributed and linked to their sources. The analysis and opinions are the author's own.

What it is

The Association for Financial Markets in Europe, the Loan Market Association and UK Finance published AI in Wholesale Financial Services, a paper addressed to third parties that provide AI products, or use AI to deliver services, to financial institutions. Its eight recommendations are: disclose the use of AI in service delivery and in the supplier's own supply chain; give transparent, accurate disclosure of what the AI does; be transparent about model architecture; disclose clearly how the institution's data and metadata are used; include the ability to "toggle" AI functionality; enable bring-your-own-AI and bring-your-own-key models where appropriate; decouple AI features from unrelated releases; and give adequate notice of planned AI releases.

The paper is careful about what it is not asking for. It says suppliers need not disclose source code or sensitive intellectual property by default, need not treat every use of AI as "high-risk", need not adopt one set of controls regardless of the service, and need not avoid AI altogether. The stated aim is to make suppliers' services easier for financial institutions to adopt, given their regulatory obligations.

Why it matters

The problem behind recommendations five, seven and eight is AI arriving by software update. A bank assesses a supplier, signs a contract and puts the service into production. Months later, a routine release adds an AI feature that reads the bank's data. Unless the bank can switch it off, and unless it arrives on its own rather than bundled with a security patch the bank must take, the risk assessment on file describes a product that no longer exists.

If AI can arrive in a routine software update, last year's risk assessment was of a different product.

In my view, procurement and vendor-risk teams should turn these eight points into contract clauses at the next renewal, starting with the toggle and the separate release. They cost suppliers little and give the bank a way to say "not yet" without leaving the service. The data-use disclosure matters most for wholesale banks, whose metadata about clients and trades is itself sensitive. And the bring-your-own-key option lets a bank keep using the model it has already approved, instead of whichever model the supplier switched to this quarter. Issue 08 found that fewer than half of large banks have a tool-based inventory of their AI; these clauses are how AI inside bought software gets into that inventory.

What I'd watch

  • Whether large software suppliers to banks add AI toggles and separate AI release notes.
  • Whether supervisors cite the paper in their own expectations for managing third-party AI.
  • Whether trade bodies outside Europe publish similar lists for their members' suppliers.
