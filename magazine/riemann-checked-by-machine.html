A proof of the Riemann hypothesis written with Claude passed every machine check in a day. Understanding it will take longer

On 20 November Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge released a proof of the 167-year-old Riemann hypothesis as a 17-million-line Lean library, written with Claude over about six weeks. The next day the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative said they had full confidence it is correct. The speed came from the checking, and the part that needed people was making sure the right statement was proved.

By Shashwat Kumar · 2026-11-24 · 5 min read · Analysis

Facts are attributed and linked to their sources. The analysis and opinions are the author's own.

What it is

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, posted a note on his website on Friday 20 November saying Claude had helped him prove the Riemann hypothesis, the Guardian reported, over about six weeks. The hypothesis, proposed by Bernhard Riemann in 1859, concerns how the prime numbers are distributed and is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, for each of which the Clay Mathematics Institute offers $1m. Alpöge released the proof not as a paper but as a library in Lean, a programming language in which mathematical proofs can be checked by computer.

On 21 November the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative issued a joint statement: "We have full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct." They said four criteria were met. The statement being proved is Mathlib's own, vetted by experts in Lean and number theory. Dozens of people had compiled the 17-million-line library without errors and reproduced the same proof export. Two independent tools confirmed that the theorem Alpöge proved is exactly Mathlib's statement of the hypothesis. And several proof checkers accepted the exported proof, including Lean's official kernel and two checkers, con-leche and con-ron, that come with a formal proof that they cannot accept a false proof. The checks ran on an inert export of the proof, out of reach of any malicious code in the sources.

MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland, one of those who compiled it independently, told Scientific American: "Honestly the chance that anything is fishy here is approximately zero." The note came a day after Anthropic's shares began trading on Nasdaq; Alpöge wrote that "the precise timing is horrible."

Why it matters

The striking number is not the 17 million lines but the one day. A traditional proof of this size would take referees years. This one was accepted quickly because checking it was mechanical and could be done by many people and many checkers at once. The hard human work was the specification: making sure the theorem proved was the real Riemann hypothesis and not a subtly different claim, and that nothing in the code redefined the symbols in the statement. Two of the four criteria are about exactly that.

The machine checked seventeen million lines in a day. The part people had to get right was one line: what exactly was being proved.

That is the lesson for anyone outside mathematics. Issue 09 covered Google DeepMind verifying an encryption library down to the machine code, and Issue 03 argued for proofs over patches. As AI writes more of the proofs, tests and controls a firm relies on, the scarce skill becomes stating precisely what must be true and checking that the evidence proves that statement and not a neighbour of it. In my view a bank asking an AI to show that a control works should spend most of its review time on the wording of the claim.

The proof also leaves things undone. A verified Lean library tells you the result is true; it does not explain why. The Lean groups say they look forward to work that will "help explain the new ideas in this proof". Until mathematicians can read and teach those ideas, the result is certain but not yet understood.

What I'd watch

  • A human-readable account of the proof's new ideas, and how mathematicians judge them.
  • How the Clay Mathematics Institute treats a machine-verified proof written with an AI system.
  • Whether Anthropic says how much compute and what kind of model access the six weeks needed.
  • Whether the same verify-first approach reaches software and financial controls, where the statement to prove is harder to write.
