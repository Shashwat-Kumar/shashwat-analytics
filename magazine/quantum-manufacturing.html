Quantum's factory turn

IonQ, Quantinuum, IBM's Anderon and D-Wave all made manufacturing news in five weeks. When an industry starts talking about tapeouts and wafer lots, it is preparing to be measured like one.

By Shashwat Kumar · 2026-10-13 · 2 min read · Analysis

Facts are attributed and linked to their sources. The analysis and opinions are the author's own.

What it is

On 8 September IonQ launched Superion 256, its sixth-generation platform with 256 physical trapped-ion qubits. IonQ says its first fully integrated 256-qubit chips were fabricated at its subsidiary SkyWater, that the chip went through six tapeouts in the first half of 2026, and that its design cycle compressed from nine months to two. Superion 256 is available to order, with customer deliveries in 2027.

The same day Quantinuum finalised a $100 million CHIPS R&D award with the U.S. Department of Commerce to support manufacturing for trapped-ion systems, partnering with GlobalFoundries on 300mm-wafer ion traps and electronics and with Monarch Quantum on lasers and optical components. On 16 September Anderon, an IBM company, finalised a $1 billion CHIPS award to accelerate R&D for its 300-millimetre pure-play quantum wafer foundry in Albany, New York.

On 13 October SkyWater and D-Wave announced a multi-year manufacturing agreement. They also reported that an earlier proof-of-technology project, using D-Wave's annealing system to optimise a maintenance schedule at SkyWater's fab, reduced lot delays by up to 30%.

Why it matters

Manufacturing language is a sign of maturity. Physics demonstrations happen once; products have to be made repeatedly, with yields, supply chains and lead times. Public money flowing into foundries means governments believe the bottleneck is now partly industrial, not only scientific.

When an industry starts talking about tapeouts and wafer lots, it is preparing to be measured like one.

It also brings the measures of manufacturing with it: tapeouts, wafer lots, delivery dates. Those are more verifiable than headline qubit counts, which is good for anyone trying to assess progress honestly.

The D-Wave example deserves the most careful reading, because it is the kind of claim a business audience will hear first. A reduction in lot delays is a real operational outcome, which is exactly the right sort of metric. The question any analytics team would ask next is the counterfactual: compared with what? A schedule optimised by a strong classical method, or the previous manual process? The release describes a proof of technology and a forthcoming proof of concept with more constraints, which is an honest framing; the comparison is what will make it convincing.

For buyers, the manufacturing turn changes how to engage. Instead of evaluating science, you start evaluating vendors: roadmap credibility, supply-chain resilience, upgrade paths and service levels. IonQ's emphasis on an upgradeable platform and on cloud access to production systems is a response to precisely that buyer concern.

For financial institutions in particular, the near-term relevance is less about running workloads and more about planning: understanding when cryptography will need to change, and which optimisation problems might plausibly benefit first. Neither requires owning a machine.

What I'd watch

  • Error rates per delivered system, not only qubit counts per announced system.
  • Whether 2027 delivery dates hold; delivery is the most honest metric a hardware company has.
  • Applied optimisation claims like the 30% lot-delay reduction: what was the classical baseline, and would a good conventional solver have done the same?
