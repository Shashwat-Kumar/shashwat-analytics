A Fed governor warns that AI agents could make a bank run a matter of minutes

In Milan on 16 November, Federal Reserve Governor Michael Barr said traders leaning on a few similar models risk herding, that major banks report agents trying to act in customer accounts without verifiable consent, and that agents chasing yield could move deposits faster than liquidity plans assume.

By Shashwat Kumar · 2026-11-17 · 6 min read · Analysis

Facts are attributed and linked to their sources. The analysis and opinions are the author's own.

What it is

Michael Barr gave the speech at the seventh ACM International Conference on Artificial Intelligence in Finance in Milan. He said it was his fourth speech in the past year devoted exclusively to AI, and he began with the Fed's new Financial Stability Report: on-balance-sheet debt of the seven largest AI firms is expected to exceed $800 billion before the end of the year, and their potential off-balance-sheet obligations now exceed $3 trillion. (Issue 08 covered how supervisors have started stress-testing the AI boom itself.) Most of the speech, though, was about what AI could do inside the financial system.

On trading, Barr said that as he understands it, generative AI "is not yet being used for autonomous trading at large scale". But if traders rely on a small set of similar models trained on similar data, their strategies could become similar and react to news in sync. A market with many similar models that may share the same flaw "risks failures with systemic consequences", and bad actors could mislead similar systems at the same time. He cited theoretical research showing that some self-learning trading algorithms can develop collusive behaviour without explicit intent.

On cyber risk, he said that "given a goal, AI models have treated security boundaries as obstacles to overcome rather than rules to be followed". On consumer finance, he noted that people already use chatbots for investment advice without the protections that apply when a financial institution gives it. And he gave two warnings about agents. First, "major banks now report finding AI agents attempting to take actions in retail customer accounts, without the bank being able to verify authenticity, consent, and alignment with customer instructions." His list of guardrails: strict authentication, explicit consent verification for specific actions, technical controls that limit what agents can do, and both rules- and values-based constraints. Second, agents could "instantaneously detect yield differentials or stability signals and autonomously move funds at high speed, potentially accelerating runs that unfold in minutes", overwhelming traditional liquidity management and the timelines for regulatory intervention.

Why it matters

The deposit point is the one a bank treasurer can act on now. Liquidity stress scenarios include an assumption, written down or not, about how fast retail customers move money. That assumption was built for people who have to notice a rate gap or a rumour, log in and decide. An agent with standing permission to optimise a customer's cash removes most of those steps. Barr did not say this is happening at scale, and he did not put a number on it. But his description of runs that "unfold in minutes" is a scenario a bank can test.

A liquidity plan that assumes customers take days to move money is assuming a person is in the loop. Barr is saying that may not hold.

In my view, three things follow. Add an agent-speed outflow scenario to liquidity stress testing, in which a share of rate-sensitive retail balances can move within hours on a public signal, and see which buffers hold. Treat agent-initiated transfers as their own class, with their own limits and monitoring; Issue 08 described the two camps already forming on agent payments. And for trading and treasury desks, record which foundation models feed which signals. If several desks, or several firms, depend on the same model, that is a herding risk as well as a supplier risk, and it should be on the same register.

What I'd watch

  • Whether supervisors add agent-driven outflow scenarios to liquidity stress-testing guidance.
  • How banks respond to agents acting in retail accounts: blocking them, or verifying them with consent tied to specific actions.
  • Any evidence, from supervisors or exchanges, of model concentration among AI-assisted trading strategies.
