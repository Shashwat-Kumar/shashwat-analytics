CASE 02 · PREDICTIVE ANALYTICS

Contoso Play: is the better churn model worth deploying?

In October 2026, Ananya Rao, head of subscriber retention at Contoso Play, must decide how to act on a new churn model before 38,000 subscribers reach the end of an introductory price. Her data science team proposes a full campaign that it expects to earn ₹4.1 crore a year. The finance team wants evidence that retention offers work at all.

01 / INTRODUCTION

A stronger model and a deadline

On Thursday, 22 October 2026, Ananya Rao had two documents on her desk. The first was a proposal from Karthik Subramanian, who led Contoso Play's data science team, to replace the company's churn model with a more accurate one and use it to run a retention campaign across the subscriber base. Subramanian estimated that the campaign would add ₹68 lakh of value in every 60-day cycle, or about ₹4.1 crore a year. The second was a note from Neha Kapoor, the chief financial officer, reminding Rao that last year's win-back campaign had cost ₹1.1 crore and that no one had been able to say whether it had kept a single subscriber.

Rao had until the end of the month to decide. In August, 38,000 subscribers had joined on a three-month introductory price of ₹99 a month. In November their price would rise to the standard rate, and the data science team's own analysis showed that subscribers whose promotion was about to end were among the most likely to leave.

02 / THE INDUSTRY

Subscription video in India

India's market for subscription video had grown quickly but remained intensely competitive. Large global services, broadcaster-owned platforms and specialist regional services competed for the same households, often with low introductory prices, bundles with telecom plans and exclusive sports or film rights. Subscribers could cancel at any time and frequently moved between services to follow a particular series or tournament. For most services, retaining an existing subscriber cost less than acquiring a new one, and the speed at which subscribers left was among the most closely watched measures of the business.

Retention teams typically had a small set of tools: reminders to subscribers whose payments had failed, recommendations of content likely to interest a lapsing viewer, offers to move to a cheaper plan, and discounts. Discounts were the most expensive, because they were taken up by subscribers who would have stayed anyway as well as by those who would have left.

03 / THE COMPANY

Contoso Play

Contoso Play was launched in Mumbai in 2020 as a streaming service for Hindi and regional-language films and series. By October 2026 it had about 2.4 lakh paying subscribers across three plans: Basic at ₹199 a month, Standard at ₹399 and Premium at ₹649, with annual plans priced at ten months' fees. The average subscriber paid about ₹400 a month. Roughly 14.5% of subscribers cancelled or let their subscription lapse in any 60-day period. Subscribers on monthly plans, those who paid manually by UPI rather than by automatic debit, and those whose promotional price was about to end left at the highest rates.

Retention sat within the marketing function. Rao's team of eleven ran payment reminders, content campaigns and offers through the company's customer relationship management (CRM) system, which could send unlimited push notifications and e-mails; a contracted call centre could make about 6,000 retention calls in each 60-day cycle. Since 2025 the CRM had scored every subscriber each night with a logistic-regression churn model built by Subramanian's team. The score was used to prioritise calls, but no campaign had ever been run with a control group.

04 / THE PROBLEM

A better model, and an untested assumption

In September, Subramanian's team had built a new model. They took a snapshot of 10,000 subscribers, recorded each subscriber's behaviour up to that date and noted whether the subscriber left in the following 60 days. They trained a gradient-boosting model on 70% of the records and tested it on the remaining 3,000. On the test records the new model scored an AUC of 0.797, against 0.788 for the existing logistic model (Exhibit 1).1 The 10% of subscribers it ranked as riskiest left at 3.55 times the average rate, and the riskiest 30% included 68.2% of all subscribers who left (Exhibit 2).

Subramanian's proposal set out how many subscribers to contact. Scaling the test results to 100,000 subscribers, it estimated the cost and benefit of offering two months at half price to subscribers above different score thresholds (Exhibit 4). Net value peaked when the riskiest 15% were contacted, at ₹28.5 lakh per 100,000 subscribers per cycle; across 2.4 lakh subscribers that came to about ₹68 lakh a cycle. The estimate rested on three assumptions: that the offer would keep 35% of the would-be leavers who received it, that half of those who would have stayed anyway would also accept it, and that a retained subscriber was worth six months of fees. None of the three had been measured at Contoso.

Deploying the new model would also cost money. The logistic model ran inside the CRM as a nightly query. The gradient-boosting model would need a new scoring pipeline in the data warehouse, monitoring for drift, and a review by the company's model-risk reviewer before going live. The data engineering team estimated ₹18 lakh and six weeks, which meant the new scores could not be used before early December, after the August cohort's price had risen.

05 / THE POSITIONS

Four views of the same model

Four executives had a stake in the decision (Exhibit 5).

Karthik Subramanian, head of data science, wanted the new model in production. "It is better on every measure we track, and the economics are positive at every threshold up to half the base," he said. He accepted that the 35% save rate was an assumption, but argued that the campaign was profitable even if the true figure were lower, and that delaying it would cost far more than the pipeline.

Neha Kapoor, chief financial officer, did not dispute the model. She disputed the claim that contacting a subscriber changed what the subscriber did. "A model that predicts who will leave tells me nothing about who can be persuaded to stay," she told Rao. "Last year we gave half-price months to forty thousand people and we still do not know whether we kept any of them. I will fund a campaign that can prove its effect. I will not fund another one that cannot."

Farhan Ali, head of campaign operations, was concerned with practicalities. A randomised test would require him to hold back a share of high-risk subscribers from any offer, which the sales team had resisted in the past, and the call centre's 6,000 calls per cycle covered only a fraction of the riskiest 15%. He also pointed out that the August cohort included many subscribers on the Basic plan, for whom a two-month discount was worth very little.

Ananya Rao, head of subscriber retention, owned the budget and the result. She wanted to protect the August cohort, but she also knew that a second campaign without measurable results would end the programme.

AFTER YOUR DECISIONANALYSIS & CASE OUTCOME

AFTER THE DECISION / ANALYSIS

Question 1: the gain in accuracy changes little

The new model's AUC is 0.009 higher, less than one percentage point, and the test set of 3,000 records is too small to show that the difference is reliable without a confidence interval for paired scores. More importantly, the two models select 86% of the same subscribers for the riskiest 15%. Adopting the new model would change the treatment of about one targeted subscriber in seven, at a cost of ₹18 lakh, ongoing monitoring and a six-week delay that misses the November price change. Students who favour option A should be asked to show what the new model would earn that the existing one would not.

Question 2: the save rate decides the result

Exhibit 4 assumes that the offer keeps 35% of would-be leavers who receive it. Using the proposal's own cost structure, the campaign at the riskiest 15% breaks even at a save rate of about 14% (Exhibit TN1). Below that it loses money, because half of the subscribers who would have stayed also take the discount. Nothing in the case indicates whether the true rate is nearer 35% or 10%. The model predicts who will leave; it says nothing about who will respond. Some high-risk subscribers leave whatever they are offered, and some lower-risk subscribers can be persuaded. Ascarza (2018) shows that targeting the customers most likely to leave can be less effective than targeting those most likely to respond.

Enter a scenario to calculate estimated net contribution.
Net contribution = expected leavers among those selected × reach rate × share retained × (contribution − incentive) − subscribers selected × contact cost. The calculation assumes churn risk does not vary with reachability, and excludes taxes, fixed costs, discounting, differences in response between subscribers, and uncertainty.

Question 3: the control group is cheap evidence

Holding back 20% of the riskiest 15% from any offer gives up 20% of the campaign's value. At the proposal's own assumptions that is about ₹5.7 lakh per 100,000 subscribers, or about ₹14 lakh per cycle across 2.4 lakh subscribers. If the true save rate is lower than 35%, the cost of the control group is lower too, and below the break-even rate the control group saves money. In return Contoso learns the one number on which the business case depends. Students should compare this with the ₹1.1 crore spent last year without any measurement.

Question 4: test now with the model already in production

The case contains a resolution that students often miss: the existing logistic model already scores every subscriber each night. A randomised test can therefore start in November, before the August cohort's price rises, without waiting for the new pipeline. The question of the new model can be deferred until the test shows whether the offers work at all.

AUTHOR'S RECOMMENDATION

Start a randomised test in the first week of November using the existing model: target the riskiest 15%, with the August cohort first, and hold back 20% at random as a control. Match the offer to the main reason in each score, such as a payment reminder, a content recommendation or a plan change, and reserve discounts for Standard and Premium subscribers. Measure 60-day retention and contribution net of offer costs. Defer the new model until the test shows whether its additional targets would earn more than ₹18 lakh.

WHAT HAPPENED NEXT / READING NOTES

Epilogue

Rao launched a randomised test on 3 November using the existing model, with the August cohort first and a 20% control group. By mid-January the measured save rate was well below the 35% assumed in the proposal but above break-even for the riskiest 10% of subscribers. Contoso narrowed its target group, replaced most discounts with plan changes and content recommendations, and deferred the new model to the following financial year.
