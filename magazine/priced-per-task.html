OpenAI and Google both sold their new models this week on fewer tokens per task. Measure the task, not the token

GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol arrived on 17 November and Gemini 4 Neon on 18 November. Both launches lead with cost per finished task rather than price per token, and Google's own numbers show the software around a model can triple its score. Buyers should change their unit of measure to match.

By Shashwat Kumar · 2026-11-24 · 5 min read · Analysis

Facts are attributed and linked to their sources. The analysis and opinions are the author's own.

What it is

On 17 November OpenAI introduced GPT-6.2 Astra, its frontier model for the hardest work, and GPT-6.2 Sol for everyday tasks. Its headline claim is efficiency: on Agents' Last Exam, a test of multistep professional work, Astra scores 5.2 points higher than GPT-6 Astra "at less than a third of the cost", and on OpenAI's internal GDPval v2 it gains 6.2 points while using 22% fewer output tokens. Astra's API prices are 20% lower than GPT-6 Astra's: $8 per million input tokens, $0.40 cached and $40 output. Sol keeps GPT-6.1 Sol's prices. OpenAI says both models meet the Critical threshold for cybersecurity under its Preparedness Framework and are launching with tighter safeguards that "also restrict some legitimate vulnerability research".

A day later Google introduced Gemini 4 Neon, which sits between Gemini 4 Carbon and Hydrogen. It costs $3 per million input tokens ($0.15 cached) and $15 output, with a 1-million-token context window, and Google says it scores 61 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at its highest thinking level, third overall. At its default level, Google says, it has a 32% lower cost per task than Carbon and roughly 50% to 80% lower cost per task than top competing frontier models on two indexes. A cyber-defence version is going to vetted defenders through Google's Fairwind Program.

Why it matters

Both companies are telling buyers the same thing: list price per token is the wrong number. A model that thinks longer, rereads files or needs more turns can cost more per finished job even at a lower token price, and the reverse is also true. Google's post includes a clear illustration of a second variable. On one coding benchmark, Neon scores 12.8% in a minimal test harness and 39.0% inside Google's own Antigravity tools. The same model, wrapped in different software, more than tripled its result.

A cheaper token can still buy a dearer answer. The unit that matters is the finished task, measured on your own work.

Almost all the figures in both launches are benchmarks the vendors chose, many run by the vendors. That does not make them wrong, but it means they are claims. Issue 08 made the related point that the price that matters is the one after the promotion. In my view the practical response is a small internal test set of real tasks, scored for correctness and for total cost per completed task at each thinking level, rerun whenever a model changes. It is the only comparison that includes your prompts, your tools and your error tolerance.

What I'd watch

  • Independent cost-per-task measurements for GPT-6.2 Astra, Sol and Gemini 4 Neon on the same tasks.
  • How often the tighter cyber safeguards on GPT-6.2 block legitimate security work, and when the models reach OpenAI's Daybreak programme for defenders.
  • Whether enterprise contracts start to price by completed task or outcome rather than by token.
