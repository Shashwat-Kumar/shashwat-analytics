Humanoid robots clocked in at China's post office and a phone supplier's test line this week. The numbers that would prove them are mostly missing

ROBOTERA said its humanoids now work in 11 China Post hubs across Guangdong, AgiBot put dozens of robots on Lens Technology's phone test lines, and GlobalFoundries signed up to test Boston Dynamics' Atlas in its chip fabs. The four-legged inspection robot in the same announcement is the only one with a full set of operating numbers.

By Shashwat Kumar · 2026-11-24 · 4 min read · Analysis

Facts are attributed and linked to their sources. The analysis and opinions are the author's own.

What it is

On 17 November ROBOTERA said its humanoid robots are deployed across China Post's Guangdong network, at 11 postal hubs in 8 cities, which it calls the largest and most geographically extensive deployment of embodied robots in logistics so far. It says that since a pilot began at the Guangzhou Postal District Center in March, its robots have averaged more than 1,500 parcels an hour at induction, "comparable to the performance of experienced human workers". The release does not say how many robots are deployed.

On 20 November AgiBot put dozens of its Genie G2 robots on Lens Technology's phone test lines, where they load and unload test stations, Robrief reported, citing Jiemian and National Business Daily. Lens, which The Paper describes as Apple's leading supplier in China, also assembles AgiBot's humanoids, so the line tests the robots inside their maker's own supply chain. The reports give no throughput.

Also on 17 November Boston Dynamics and GlobalFoundries said Spot, the company's four-legged inspection robot, will expand across GlobalFoundries fabs in New York, Vermont, Dresden and Singapore, and that GlobalFoundries will be the first customer outside the car industry to test the Atlas humanoid, from early 2027. The release gives Spot's numbers: in Singapore, two robots have travelled nearly 900 km and completed more than 90,000 inspections in sub-fab areas since April, with a 99% mission success rate.

Why it matters

Issue 09 covered a humanoid kitting car parts at 96% success, a rare case with a published success rate. This week's announcements are larger in scale and thinner in evidence. A parcel rate per robot without a robot count, or "dozens" of robots with no output figure, cannot be turned into a business case. The quadruped doing inspections is the only one of the three with distance, task count and success rate.

A deployment without a robot count is a press release. Ask how many robots, for how many hours, with how many human rescues.

In my view anyone evaluating physical AI, including insurers and lenders financing it, should ask for the same four numbers each time: how many units, hours in service, success rate per task, and how often a person had to step in. Robots that work alongside their own manufacturers, as at Lens, should be read as pilots until the same results appear at an unrelated customer.

What I'd watch

  • Robot counts and uptime from the China Post deployment through the peak shopping season, when ROBOTERA says parcel volumes can surge to several times normal.
  • Whether AgiBot repeats the Lens set-up at a phone maker with no ownership or manufacturing tie.
  • The tasks GlobalFoundries chooses for Atlas in 2027, and whether it publishes results as it has for Spot.
