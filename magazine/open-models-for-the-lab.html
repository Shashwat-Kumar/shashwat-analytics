NVIDIA's open science model beat a frontier model on one lab task at a fraction of the cost. That is the case for owning a model

At the SC26 supercomputing conference, NVIDIA released Nemotron for Science: its open Nemotron 3.5 Super VL model, an open chemistry dataset and recipes for post-training. Its customers' results make the argument for tuning a smaller model on your own outcomes rather than renting the largest one.

By Shashwat Kumar · 2026-11-17 · 4 min read · Analysis

Facts are attributed and linked to their sources. The analysis and opinions are the author's own.

What it is

On 16 November NVIDIA introduced Nemotron for Science, built on its Nemotron 3.5 Super VL open model. It includes scientific evaluations, post-training recipes and LitmusBench v0.2, a new open dataset of nearly 50,000 chemistry-reasoning questions. The model and dataset are on Hugging Face, and the model is optimised to run locally on a 128GB DGX Spark desktop system. NVIDIA gave several customer results. Lila Sciences post-trained the model with laboratory feedback and nearly doubled its accuracy at producing synthesis recipes for quantum dots, to 61%, which NVIDIA says exceeded GPT-6 Astra's 54% at 10 to 20 times lower inference cost. Terray Therapeutics recovered about 76% of annotated reactions on the RxnScribe test. A KTH Royal Institute of Technology agent built on Nemotron fixed 81% of broken GROMACS molecular-dynamics setups overnight on a single DGX Spark, with every fix checked independently by GROMACS.

The rest of NVIDIA's SC26 news was hardware. The Vera CPU rack-scale platform combines 192 Vera CPUs and is expected from Dell Technologies, HPE, Lenovo and Supermicro from early 2027. Rubin HPC, a GPU with more than 50,000 CUDA cores, is expected in 2028. And a DGX Spark HPC Developer Kit links four DGX Spark systems into a desk-side cluster with 512GB of combined memory, which NVIDIA says can run models of up to 700 billion parameters locally.

Why it matters

The Lila result is one task, reported by the vendor, and should be read that way. But the pattern is the important part, and it is not limited to chemistry. A smaller open model, tuned on an organisation's own outcome data and run on hardware it controls, outperformed a general frontier model on a narrow task at a fraction of the running cost. Banks have the same kind of narrow, outcome-rich tasks, such as document extraction, fraud triage and collections, and the same reasons to keep data in-house.

The interesting number is not 61% against 54%. It is that the 61% came from the lab's own feedback, on a model the lab gets to keep.

The KTH example adds the control that makes this safe to try: every fix the agent proposed was checked by the simulation software itself before it counted. In my view, that is the right design for any agent doing technical work. Let it propose as much as it likes, and accept only what an independent check confirms. For a bank, the equivalent is a reconciliation or a rules engine, not a person rereading the agent's output.

What I'd watch

  • Independent results on LitmusBench v0.2 from models other than NVIDIA's.
  • Whether the Lila and Therna results hold up in published, peer-reviewed form.
  • Independent benchmarks of the Vera CPU platform beyond NVIDIA's own tests.
