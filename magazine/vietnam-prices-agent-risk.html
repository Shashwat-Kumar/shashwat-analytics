Vietnam has put a money value on when a banking AI becomes high-risk. India's market regulator says its own rules are coming

From 15 November, an AI system that pays on its own for an individual Vietnamese bank customer is high-risk above VND 10 million a transaction or VND 20 million a day, and agent payments are capped at VND 100 million a day. Customers must sign a biometrically verified agreement that sets the limits. A day earlier, SEBI's chairman said India's AI guidelines for capital markets will come soon, with a human in the loop for critical decisions.

By Shashwat Kumar · 2026-11-16 · 6 min read · Analysis

Facts are attributed and linked to their sources. The analysis and opinions are the author's own.

What it is

The State Bank of Vietnam issued Circular 53/2026/TT-NHNN on 30 September, and it took effect on 15 November. It covers banks, foreign bank branches, payment intermediaries, mobile-money providers and credit-information companies that use AI to make or support decisions in customer services. Article 4 draws hard money lines. An AI system that automatically executes payments is high-risk if it handles an individual customer's transaction above VND 10 million, or more than VND 20 million in a day; for organisations the lines are VND 50 million and VND 100 million. An AI system that automatically makes credit decisions is high-risk at VND 50 million or more.

Article 5 sets the conditions for letting AI act on its own. Automated execution is allowed only where no law requires a human decision, the models have passed the institution's quality thresholds, risk thresholds and monitoring are defined, a human can intervene, and it is possible to tell whether a transaction was carried out by an AI system or a person. For agent payments, the customer must sign an agreement, verified electronically by at least a biometric match plus another factor, that states the scope, per-transaction and daily limits, validity period and suspension conditions, and the customer's right to end it at any time. Whatever the agreement says, AI-executed payments may not exceed VND 100 million a day for an individual or VND 500 million for an organisation. Customers must get a tool to suspend or revoke the authority. Within the agreement, payments do not need the usual per-transaction confirmation.

Three other clauses stand out. Article 3 says institutions may not cite "the technical characteristics or autonomy" of an AI system, or a third party's results, to deny or reduce their liability. Article 8 requires institutions using third-party models through an API for higher-risk decisions to have impact assessments and contingency plans for when the provider updates the model. Article 18 requires vendors to promise not to train on the institution's data without written consent. Before going live, a system needs a dossier approved by what the circular calls the institution's competent authority, which Article 2 defines as its own board or equivalent. Some provisions phase in during 2027, and systems already in production have until 1 March 2027.

In India, SEBI chairman Tuhin Kanta Pandey said on 14 November that the regulator will "soon" issue detailed guidelines on the responsible use of AI in capital markets, PTI reported. He said they would cover disclosures, the accountability of regulated entities and a "human-in-the-loop" approach so that critical decisions stay under human oversight. No draft text was released. The starting point is SEBI's June 2025 consultation paper, which proposed testing in an environment separate from live systems, shadow testing with live traffic, and keeping models' input and output data for at least five years.

Why it matters

This is the most concrete rule for agent payments I have read from a banking regulator. It does not ask whether a model is clever. It asks how much money the AI can move, for whom, per day, under what signed mandate, and whether anyone can tell afterwards that a machine did it. That is the same question the card industry is fighting over in the US, answered differently: instead of a fingerprint on every purchase, one strongly verified agreement with limits, a daily cap and a revoke button.

Vietnam does not regulate how clever the model is. It regulates how much money it can move, for whom, per day, and under what signed mandate.

The daily totals are the detail builders will feel. An agent making many small payments can cross the VND 20 million daily line for an individual without any single payment looking large, so aggregation per customer per day has to be designed in, not added later. The model-update clause matters too: a firm running a decision on a vendor's API now needs a plan for the day the vendor changes the model underneath it.

For Indian firms, SEBI's comments are a signal, not yet a rule; Issue 03 covered an earlier step in "India's market regulator wants a kill switch". But the pieces Vietnam has written down are useful whatever text SEBI publishes: a register of AI systems with risk ratings, a record of every human override, logs that can reproduce a decision, and a mandate object that states scope, limits, validity and how to revoke it, in a form both a machine and an auditor can read.

What I'd watch

  • The text of SEBI's guidelines, and whether they set any limits on what AI may execute without a human.
  • Whether the RBI or NPCI says anything comparable about mandates for AI-initiated payments.
  • How Vietnamese banks implement the biometric agreement, and whether the daily caps are revised once there is data.
