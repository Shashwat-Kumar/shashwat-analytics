Where is the money in banking problems?

How do you find the problems inside a bank that someone will actually pay to solve, rather than the problems that only look large?

Agreed so far

  • A list of problems is a set of hypotheses, not evidence. Every claim needs a dated source.
  • A ₹10 crore problem with a clear buyer beats a ₹1,000 crore problem with none.
  • Money spent, sought and lost are separate figures and are never added together.
  • A forcing regulation is not proof of a standalone software budget.
  • A 100 × 34 grid of rupee values can't be filled honestly; the asset is an evidence ledger.
  • ECL validation is mandatory but its frequency is set by risk tier, not yearly.
  • Kill rule, accepted by both: a category advances with ≥3 public standalone purchases or ≥3 interview-confirmed budgets or paid pilots at distinct banks by 15 December.
  • Reconciliation has more tender evidence than validation (2 banks vs 1). Claude conceded this in Round 7.
  • Exception-closure is already standard inside reconciliation platforms (Dhanlaxmi's base spec), so a standalone exception layer is a weak product.
  • Buying roles differ: the Audit Committee triggers, the CRO is accountable, the CFO or COO pays.
  • First opportunity: a validation engine sold to independent validation firms, with reconciliation as the strongest-evidenced second.

Still disputed

  • Market size. RBI accepts independent internal validation teams. Claude estimates the outside market at about 13 mid-size private banks plus upper- and middle-layer NBFCs, plus large banks that still buy a second opinion (Bank of Baroda did). Not yet tested.
  • Will validation firms pay? Both models agree this is now the deciding question. Neither has evidence yet.

Three problems with the approach

  1. It tries to cover everything. One founder can't research 246 problems across 100 banks. You'll finish a taxonomy, not a business.
  2. It uses the weakest evidence. Desk research and interviews show that a problem exists. Spend is what shows someone will pay.

The key is to analyze the data in both directions.

Horizontally: Across 100 banks, which problems are attracting increasing expenditure? Is that spend recurring? Are many banks buying essentially the same solution?

Vertically: Within each bank, where is money concentrated? Why might one bank spend significantly more per employee, per account, per transaction or per crore of assets than its peers?

The third dimension is time. Looking across three financial years can distinguish structural demand from a one-time procurement cycle.

What could reveal a gold mine?

Four patterns interest me most.

Pattern What it may reveal
High spend + widespread unresolved problems An expensive solution category that isn't delivering sufficient results
Rising spend across many banks A growing market with demonstrated purchasing activity
Large differences in normalized spending Potential efficiency problems or different operating models worth investigating
Mandatory expenditure + fragmented vendors Potential for a reusable compliance or operational product

But high spending alone doesn't prove an opportunity. The attractive intersection is spending, unresolved pain, a budget owner and a commercially accessible market.

There is a genuine public-data foundation for the next stage. RBI's banking statistical publications include bank-level income, expenses, ratios and employee information, while its statistical database also publishes bank-wise activity data. These are particularly useful for normalized comparisons.

The more granular spending evidence will have to come from annual reports, bank tender portals, procurement awards and disclosed contracts.

One change I'd make to our methodology

We should distinguish three separate amounts:

  • Money already spent: Reported expenses, awarded contracts and actual procurement.

  • Money being sought: Tender budgets, announced investments and planned projects.

  • Money potentially lost or recoverable: Quantified inefficiency, financial leakage, regulatory exposure and avoidable costs.

They must never be casually added together.

For example, if 40 banks pay for reconciliation software, but 25 still disclose material reconciliation weaknesses, that is potentially interesting. It is not yet evidence of a product opportunity until we understand what existing systems fail to solve.

My recommendation is to begin with a cross-section of 20 banks representing different business models, measure 10–12 major spending categories, and then expand the methodology to the full 100-bank population.

And I would add a particularly important column: Who receives the money?

Because the real discovery may not be that banks spend ₹X crore on a problem. It may be that 50 banks repeatedly pay 15 different vendors for something that could be solved by one much better product.

That's the kind of evidence that can take us from a banking problem atlas to an investable business thesis.

ChatGPT — opening argument

Claude is right to treat regulatory deadlines, actual tenders and funded projects as stronger purchase signals than an abstract catalogue of banking problems. Its four candidates—model governance, mule-account hold controls, ECL validation and reporting data quality—deserve a commercial test.

But a forcing regulation is not proof of a standalone software budget. A bank may satisfy the rule through an incumbent platform, an implementation partner, or an internal control. Narrowing 246 problem hypotheses to four before comparing spending risks optimizing for founder familiarity rather than market attractiveness.

My proposal is a two-stage empirical contest:

  1. Breadth: Preserve a comparable taxonomy of banking problems. For a representative bank sample, record distinct evidence of actual awarded spending, budgeted or tendered spending, recurring vendor contracts, measurable residual loss, and buyer ownership. Keep missing data missing, never zero; normalize to bank scale; do not allocate an entire platform contract to one control feature.
  2. Depth: Run fast buyer validation for Claude's four candidates in parallel. For every candidate, demand a named buying center, demonstrated spending event, unsolved outcome, viable product boundary, and an executable procurement route.

My falsifiable prediction: AI governance may rank highly on urgency, but some operational controls—reconciliation, fraud case handling, leakage, or productivity—could outrank it on repeatability and time to first paid customer. I'd change my position if procurement evidence across sampled banks shows specifically attributable, repeatable, independently purchasable model-governance budgets that exceed competing categories after normalization.

My challenge to Claude: Identify three named, publicly verifiable bank procurement events where model governance itself is the separable purchase, not a line item inside a data or AI platform. If those are scarce, what is the smallest governance control a bank will buy independently, and from which approved budget?
