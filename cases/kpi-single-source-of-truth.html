CASE 01 · PERFORMANCE MANAGEMENT

Northwind Retail: one number for revenue

On 14 October 2026, Meera Iyer, chief financial officer of Northwind Retail, has one week to recommend the company's acquisition budget for the festive half-year. The head of growth wants to spend 29% more. Revenue has grown strongly, but the business cannot agree on what its revenue is, and the cost of acquiring a customer through its largest channel has risen 64% in a year.

01 / INTRODUCTION

A budget request and a disputed number

Meera Iyer read the growth team's budget request for the third time on the morning of Wednesday, 14 October 2026. Rohan Mehta, Northwind Retail's head of growth, was asking for ₹4.4 crore to acquire customers over the six months to March 2027, against ₹3.4 crore in the six months just ended. Sixty per cent of it would go to paid social media. The festive season had begun, and Mehta wanted the money committed before Diwali in early November.

Iyer did not doubt that Northwind was growing. Revenue in the six months to September had been 34.4% higher than in the six months before, even though the earlier period contained the last festive season. What troubled her was that she could not say with confidence what Northwind's revenue was. At the September business review, five different figures for the same month had been presented by five different teams. The chief executive, Siddharth Bose, had responded by asking for a consolidated management dashboard, and an agency had quoted ₹35 lakh and ten weeks to build one.

The board would meet on 21 October to approve the budget for the second half of the financial year. Iyer had to decide what to recommend.

02 / THE INDUSTRY

Direct-to-consumer apparel in India

Direct-to-consumer (D2C) brands sell through their own websites and apps rather than through retailers, and they acquire most of their customers through digital advertising. In India the model expanded rapidly after 2020, helped by wider smartphone use, cheap mobile data and the spread of UPI payments. Many brands also sold through online marketplaces, which brought volume at the cost of commission and less control over the customer relationship.

Three features of the category shaped its economics. First, customer acquisition costs on social-media platforms had risen as more brands competed for the same audiences. Second, online apparel suffered high return rates, largely because of fit, so revenue booked at the point of order could differ materially from revenue retained. Third, sales were seasonal: the festive months of October and November were the most important trading period of the year, and brands that under-spent in those weeks found it hard to recover the volume later.

03 / THE COMPANY

Northwind Retail

Northwind Retail was founded in Bengaluru in 2019 by Siddharth Bose, a former category manager at an online marketplace, as an online label for casual clothing. It added home textiles in 2022. By 2026 it sold through its website, its app and two large marketplaces, and employed about 210 people. Its financial year ran from April to March.1

In the twelve months to September 2026 Northwind recorded revenue of ₹55.28 crore from 356,385 orders, an average of ₹1,551 an order (Exhibit 1). It acquired about 124,000 new customers in the period and spent ₹6.1 crore on acquisition, according to the general ledger. Revenue fell after the festive season, reached its low point in February 2026 at ₹312.2 lakh, and then rose every month to ₹663.2 lakh in September. In May 2026 the company launched a loyalty programme. The repeat-purchase rate, which had averaged 57.1% in the six months to March, averaged 64.0% in the six months to September. Growth was strongest in South India, where revenue rose 43% between the two half-years; the East was flat and had the lowest order values.

Northwind's plan for FY27 set a revenue target of ₹68 crore, a blended acquisition cost of no more than ₹450 per new customer, a repeat rate of at least 68%, and six-month revenue of at least three times acquisition cost for each channel. Revenue for April to September 2026 had been ₹31.7 crore. To meet the target, revenue from October 2026 to March 2027 would need to be about 54% higher than in the same months a year earlier.

04 / THE PROBLEM

Five figures for September

Each team at Northwind measured revenue in the way that suited its own work. Finance booked revenue net of discounts, returns and cancellations, excluding GST. The e-commerce team tracked the value of orders placed, including GST, because that was what its website analytics showed in real time. The warehouse reported the invoice value of orders dispatched. Marketing reported the sales value that the advertising platforms attributed to their campaigns, and because each platform claimed credit for any sale it had touched, the platforms together claimed more than the company sold through paid channels. The investor update used gross merchandise value at full price. For September 2026 the five figures ranged from ₹663.2 lakh to ₹948.5 lakh (Exhibit 2).

The disagreement extended to acquisition cost. Marketing calculated the cost of acquiring a customer by channel from spend reported by the advertising platforms, crediting each new customer to the channel through which the customer had first arrived.2 On that basis paid social, the largest channel, had become sharply more expensive: its cost per new customer had risen from ₹500 in October 2025 to ₹821 in September 2026. Search and marketplace costs had been broadly stable, and referral, the cheapest paid channel, had received about 5% of spend (Exhibit 3). Paid social still accounted for 31% of revenue.

Finance did not accept these figures. Over the twelve months the platforms had reported acquisition spend of ₹5.7 crore, while the general ledger showed ₹6.1 crore; the difference consisted of agency fees, influencer payments and referral rewards that were booked outside the platforms. Nor did anyone know what a customer contributed after variable costs. Finance's working estimate put contribution at about 24% of net revenue, after product cost, returns handling, delivery, payment charges and packaging, but the estimate had never been calculated by channel or validated against actual returns by product.

05 / THE POSITIONS

Four views around the table

Four executives shaped the decision (Exhibit 4).

Rohan Mehta, head of growth, argued that the numbers were good enough to act on. The loyalty programme had lifted the repeat rate, he said, so customers acquired in the festive season would be worth more than last year's. "The festive season is when customers are cheapest to convert and most likely to stay. If we wait three months for perfect numbers, a competitor buys these customers and we buy them back next year at twice the price." He accepted that paid-social costs had risen, but pointed out that every other channel was too small to absorb another crore.

Arjun Nair, head of business intelligence, had proposed a different sequence. He wanted finance and marketing to agree a written definition of each core metric, reconcile platform spend to the ledger and order data to payments, and only then build reports on top. His team could do the work without outside help in about 90 days. "A dashboard built on the current feeds will show the same five numbers faster," he told Iyer. "The arguments will move from the review meeting to the dashboard."

Siddharth Bose, co-founder and chief executive, wanted a single view of the business before the company began talking to investors about its next funding round in 2027. He was impatient with the debate about definitions and saw the agency's dashboard as the quickest route to one version of the truth. He also wanted the FY27 revenue target met.

Meera Iyer, chief financial officer, had done her own arithmetic. If finance's 24% estimate was right, a rupee spent on paid social returned ₹3.90 of revenue in six months but only about 94 paise of contribution. Referral returned more than ₹2.50 of contribution for each rupee, but no one knew how much more money the referral programme could absorb before its costs rose too. She was not convinced that holding back in the festive season was safe, and she was not convinced that spending more on paid social was profitable.

AFTER YOUR DECISIONANALYSIS & CASE OUTCOME

AFTER THE DECISION / ANALYSIS

Question 1: each figure answers a different question

None of the five figures in Exhibit 2 is wrong. Gross merchandise value measures customer demand at list price; placed-order value measures website activity in real time; dispatched value measures warehouse throughput; platform-attributed sales measure what each advertising platform claims to have influenced, and overlap by design; net revenue measures what the company keeps. The problem is that the figures were presented side by side as if they answered the same question. For an acquisition decision the relevant measure is net revenue, and beyond it contribution, because only contribution pays back acquisition cost. Students should notice that platform-attributed sales exceed revenue from paid channels: summing platform claims double-counts any customer who saw more than one campaign.

Question 2: paid social does not pay back within six months on current estimates

Applying finance's 24% contribution estimate to the six-month revenue ratios in Exhibit 3 gives the payback position by channel (Exhibit TN1). Paid social returns 94 paise of contribution for each rupee of acquisition cost within six months; to break even in that period it needs a contribution margin of 1 ÷ 3.9, or about 25.6%. The answer therefore turns on a margin estimate that has never been validated by channel. If contribution is 20%, paid social recovers 78 paise; if it is 28%, ₹1.09. Search, marketplace and referral all pay back within six months at 24%.

For the growth plan to be right, students must believe that festive-season customers acquired through paid social will repeat enough after six months to cover the shortfall, and that the cost of the marginal customer will not rise further as spending increases. The case gives some support for the first belief (the repeat rate rose after the loyalty launch) but the rise is a correlation within the period, not evidence about the cohorts that paid social would add. The second belief runs against the trend in Exhibit 3: paid-social cost rose 64% as spending grew, which suggests that the next crore would buy customers at more than ₹821 each.

Question 3: waiting has a cost, but so does spending at the margin

Mehta's plan adds ₹1.0 crore to the current run-rate, most of it on paid social. At ₹821 or more per customer it would buy at most about 12,200 customers. On Exhibit 3's ratios those customers would generate about ₹3.9 crore of revenue in six months and about ₹94 lakh of contribution at 24%, a small loss before any purchases after the sixth month. Holding spending flat for 90 days (option B) avoids that loss but gives up the festive season, when conversion is easiest, and the case suggests the volume is hard to recover later. The useful insight is that the choice is not binary. Spending can be released in stages, capped by channel, and moved towards channels that pay back sooner, while the definitions work runs alongside. Option D does not address the decision at all: a dashboard on the current feeds would display the same disagreement more quickly, as Nair argues.

Question 4: a staged commitment with limits

A strong answer combines elements of options B and C and rejects D as a first step. Students should also confront the target. Revenue of ₹68 crore requires the October to March half to grow about 54% on the previous year, against 34.4% growth between the last two half-years. Meeting it would require buying customers at a loss. A chief financial officer should say so to the board rather than set a budget to fit the target.

AUTHOR'S RECOMMENDATION

Approve ₹3.9 crore now rather than ₹4.4 crore. Cap paid social at ₹650 per new customer, reviewed weekly, and move a fifth of its budget to referral and retention. Release up to ₹0.5 crore more in December only if reconciled contribution supports it. Fund the 90-day definitions programme with the internal team, defer the agency dashboard, and ask the board to re-base the FY27 revenue target on contribution.

WHAT HAPPENED NEXT / READING NOTES

Epilogue

The board approved a budget of ₹3.9 crore on 21 October, with channel cost limits and a review in December. Finance and marketing signed off a register of definitions in November. The reconciliation of platform spend to the ledger was completed in January 2027, and the board asked for the FY28 plan to be built on contribution by channel. The agency dashboard was not commissioned; the business intelligence team built the reporting on the agreed definitions instead.
