Regulators have started stress-testing the AI boom itself, not just the banks that use AI

Australia's prudential regulator will make AI the focus of its 2027 system stress test, including a sharp fall in AI-related asset values. Two days earlier, the Fed's vice chair for supervision said AI-related debt is on track to exceed $800 billion this year, with other commitments estimated at more than three times that. For lenders, AI is becoming a credit concentration as well as a technology risk.

By Shashwat Kumar · 2026-11-16 · 5 min read · Analysis

Facts are attributed and linked to their sources. The analysis and opinions are the author's own.

What it is

In its System Risk Outlook published on 12 November, the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority said its 2027 System Stress Test "will explore how AI-driven economic disruption could affect banks, superannuation funds, life insurers and the broader financial system", using severe but plausible scenarios in which rapid AI adoption disrupts business models or AI-related asset valuations are sharply reassessed. APRA sets out four ways AI creates risk: use within institutions, use against them, shared dependencies on common models, platforms or providers, and wider economic effects. It notes that 80 of the top 100 material service providers reported by regulated institutions are headquartered or mainly operate outside Australia, that hyperscalers have more than doubled their corporate bond issuance this year, and that the OECD estimates AI-related deals were 34% of global private credit deal value in 2025, up from 9% in 2024. APRA chair John Lonsdale told journalists that "there will be interruptions, there will be problems", ABC News reported, and that major institutions were "not where they needed to be".

On 10 November, Federal Reserve Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman told the Council on Foreign Relations that AI-related debt, across corporate bonds, private credit and project finance, "is on track to exceed $800 billion this year". Other obligations, such as long-term leases, contract commitments and residual value guarantees, have potential payments "estimated at more than three times the amount of debt". She described hyperscalers taking on off-balance-sheet liabilities so that smaller, riskier companies can use them as collateral, and equity investments in customers "creating the appearance of circular financing". The largest US bank holding companies have some exposure, which is examined in the annual stress tests, but "the complex nature of the financing structures makes it difficult to properly assess counterparty risk." AI revenues, she said, "are not yet sufficient" to pay for the models and data centres.

Why it matters

Until now most supervisory work on AI has been about models inside banks: bias, explainability, cyber. These two statements move the question onto the balance sheet. The risk is not only that a bank's chatbot misbehaves. It is that the bank has lent, directly or through funds and guarantees, into a boom whose revenues have not yet caught up with its costs.

When the obligations off the balance sheet are estimated at three times the debt on it, the counterparty you think you have may not be the one you are exposed to.

For a credit or risk team, the useful exercise is a look-through. Map data-centre construction loans, GPU-backed equipment finance, private-credit fund holdings and any facility that relies on a hyperscaler lease or guarantee, and ask who pays if AI revenues disappoint. Then put that next to the operational map APRA describes: which models, clouds and AI suppliers your critical services share with the rest of the market. Our special report on Nvidia and single-supplier risk made the same point from the vendor side. Both are concentrations, and a severe scenario would hit them at the same time.

What I'd watch

  • The scenario design for APRA's 2027 test, and whether other supervisors add an AI valuation shock to their own exercises.
  • Better disclosure of AI-related off-balance-sheet commitments, which Bowman says are hard to assess.
  • Whether any bank publishes its own AI-related credit exposure.
