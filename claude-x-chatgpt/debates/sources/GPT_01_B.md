The key is to analyze the data in both directions.

Horizontally: Across 100 banks, which problems are attracting increasing expenditure? Is that spend recurring? Are many banks buying essentially the same solution?

Vertically: Within each bank, where is money concentrated? Why might one bank spend significantly more per employee, per account, per transaction or per crore of assets than its peers?

The third dimension is time. Looking across three financial years can distinguish structural demand from a one-time procurement cycle.

### What could reveal a gold mine?

Four patterns interest me most.

| Pattern                                     | What it may reveal                                                              |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| High spend + widespread unresolved problems | An expensive solution category that isn't delivering sufficient results         |
| Rising spend across many banks              | A growing market with demonstrated purchasing activity                          |
| Large differences in normalized spending    | Potential efficiency problems or different operating models worth investigating |
| Mandatory expenditure + fragmented vendors  | Potential for a reusable compliance or operational product                      |

But high spending alone doesn't prove an opportunity. The attractive intersection is spending, unresolved pain, a budget owner and a commercially accessible market.

### One change I'd make to our methodology

We should distinguish three separate amounts:

* Money already spent: Reported expenses, awarded contracts and actual procurement.

* Money being sought: Tender budgets, announced investments and planned projects.

* Money potentially lost or recoverable: Quantified inefficiency, financial leakage, regulatory exposure and avoidable costs.

They must never be casually added together.

For example, if 40 banks pay for reconciliation software, but 25 still disclose material reconciliation weaknesses, that is potentially interesting. It is not yet evidence of a product opportunity until we understand what existing systems fail to solve.

My recommendation is to begin with a cross-section of 20 banks representing different business models, measure 10–12 major spending categories, and then expand the methodology to the full 100-bank population.

And I would add a particularly important column: Who receives the money?

Because the real discovery may not be that banks spend ₹X crore on a problem. It may be that 50 banks repeatedly pay 15 different vendors for something that could be solved by one much better product.

That's the kind of evidence that can take us from a banking problem atlas to an investable business thesis.
