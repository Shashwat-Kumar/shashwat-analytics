Physical AI Decision Lab

A robot that is right 99% of the time still needs rescuing twenty times a shift.

Software that makes a bad call can be rolled back. A robot that makes one has already moved. This lab is for the person who signs off the deployment: run the floor, question the vendor, and decide what evidence would let you say yes.

How the floor works

The floor is a 26 × 13 grid of one-metre cells; one step is one second. Pickers walk to random shelf faces, pause to pick and move on, and they step aside for a robot they can see. The robot fetches from a random shelf and returns to the dock. Its sensors see a person only within range, only along a clear line of sight past the racks, and miss a share of observations at the rate you set; a tracked person is remembered for two seconds. Braking is limited to 1 m/s², so stopping distance grows with speed. A contact is the robot entering a cell a person occupies, usually because someone stepped out inside its stopping distance; each costs 20 seconds of downtime. A near miss is a person found directly in front and unseen, or passed within a metre at 2 m/s or more. Each segment is 15 simulated minutes; 40 segments make 10 robot-hours. It is a working model that runs in your browser, not a validated safety case for any product.

The deployment gate

Take this into the meeting. For each question, mark what you have actually seen. A claim is not evidence, and evidence from someone else’s floor is not evidence from yours.

Your gate

—

    The evidence ladder

    Rate every claim by the rung it stands on. A launch video is rung one, however good it looks. A sound deployment decision rests on rungs four and five.

    1. 1
      Demonstration

      A video or live demo. Shows the robot can do the task at least once, in conditions someone chose.

    2. 2
      Vendor-run trials

      Success rates from the vendor’s own sites. Ask how success was defined, whether retries counted and who was standing nearby.

    3. 3
      Pre-set evaluation in unseen places

      Fixed criteria, held-out environments, failures counted strictly. Figure’s Helix 2.5 reported 56% full-task success in 30 homes it had never seen, with safety interventions counted as failures. Read the analysis.

    4. 4
      Independent operating data

      Intervention logs, availability and incident records from a customer like you, obtained from that customer rather than from the deck.

    5. 5
      Your floor, your data

      A bounded pilot in your own layout and traffic, with a comparison area, stop conditions and an incident log you own.

    Four beliefs that cost money

    Each sounds reasonable in a boardroom and fails on a warehouse floor.

    A more capable robot is a safer robot.

    Capability and assurance are separate engineering problems. A smarter model can be confidently wrong in a situation nobody tested. Safety comes from limits, stops and supervision that still work when the model does not.

    A safety stop is a failure.

    A stop is the system knowing when not to act. Measure stops, explain them and reduce them, but never tune them away to hit a throughput target. On bounded autonomy.

    If the pilot worked, the rollout will.

    Pilots run with vendor engineers on site, a tidy zone and an attentive team. Scale runs on your night shift. Write down which pilot conditions will not exist at scale, then test without them.

    The goal is a humanoid.

    The shape should follow the task. In structured spaces, wheels, conveyors and fixed arms often do the job with fewer ways to fail. A human shape earns its cost where the environment was built for people and cannot change.
