AI agents got their own wallet this week. America's biggest card issuer is still saying no

OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet with American Express, Mastercard and Visa: the agent never sees the card, and the user approves every purchase with a face or fingerprint check. The same day, the Wall Street Journal reported that JPMorgan Chase has been blocking agents that shop with its customers' cards. The argument is no longer whether agents will buy things, but who authorises the purchase and who carries the loss.

By Shashwat Kumar · 2026-11-16 · 6 min read · Analysis

Facts are attributed and linked to their sources. The analysis and opinions are the author's own.

What it is

On 13 November OpenAI introduced ChatGPT Wallet, built with American Express, Mastercard and Visa. It is starting in "dots", OpenAI's personal agents, for ChatGPT Pro subscribers in the US aged 18 and over with eligible US-issued cards. The user adds a card once. Before each purchase they review the items, the total and the card, then approve with a security check such as Face ID or a fingerprint, and each approval "creates a clear record of the purchase the user authorized". The payment network then issues a payment token, a substitute card number, security code and expiry date, so neither the agent nor the merchant sees the real card. OpenAI says card rewards and protections for eligible disputes carry over, and that over time it expects users to pre-approve purchases within "spending limits, purchase categories, or time periods". CNBC reports that OpenAI is not taking a cut of transactions to start.

OpenAI is also candid that the system around the wallet is not ready. Alongside the launch it published guidance for merchants, warning that some websites "may unintentionally block" agent purchases and that "risk systems may still flag legitimate purchases made by agents, leading to declines or cancellations". It names fraud-prevention partners Forter, Riskified, Sardine and Signifyd, and payment providers Adyen, Checkout.com and Stripe.

The same morning, the Wall Street Journal, republished by Mint, reported that JPMorgan Chase, the largest US card issuer by purchase volume, "has been blocking agents when consumers give the bots their card information" and has been slow to allow alternatives that rival banks are trying, according to people familiar with the matter. Stripe, by contrast, is using its Link wallet, which it says has more than 300 million users, with agentic tokens from Visa and Mastercard that carry what the consumer wants bought, purchase limits and whether the bot is trusted. For Chase cards, the paper says, Stripe has used one-time virtual cards equal to the purchase amount, then charged the customer's real card. Merchants have complained that those virtual cards are classed as commercial cards and carry higher fees. Some executives report more returns and more fraud from agent shopping, such as purchases from fake stores, and some banks plan to tell customers that authorising an agent is like handing their card to a relative or friend.

Why it matters

Put the two stories side by side and there are now three ways for an agent to pay, each with a different answer to the question that matters to a bank: who said yes? A bot holding a raw card number is the model JPMorgan is blocking, because the issuer cannot tell the agent from the customer. A one-time virtual card hides the card but moves cost onto merchants. A network token approved purchase by purchase, as in ChatGPT Wallet, keeps the customer's consent visible at the moment of payment and leaves a record of it.

The question for a card issuer is not whether to allow agents. It is whether it can tell an agent's purchase from its customer's, and what it has promised the customer when it cannot.

The liability line is the one I would watch most closely. If a bank treats an agent purchase like a card lent to a friend, a customer could lose fraud protection on a purchase they approved in principle but never saw in detail. Per-purchase biometric approval largely avoids that problem, because the customer saw the basket and the total. OpenAI's stated next step, pre-approved purchases within limits, brings it straight back. India has taken the strictest line so far: as Issue 06 reported in "Only a second channel can say yes", an agent can reach a UPI checkout, but only the shopper's PIN completes the payment.

For issuers, the practical work is the same whichever camp they join. Flag agent-initiated transactions as a separate class. Give them their own decline reasons so false declines on legitimate agent purchases can be measured, since OpenAI says these will happen. Write the dispute policy for an approved-but-unwanted agent purchase before the volume arrives, and tell customers what it says in plain words.

What I'd watch

  • Whether JPMorgan adopts network agentic tokens or joins ChatGPT Wallet, and on what terms.
  • Whether the card networks publish dispute rules that treat agent purchases as their own category.
  • When OpenAI ships pre-approved purchases, and what limits and records come with them.
