This week · updated 14 Nov 2026, 9 AM IST

  • OpenAI launched ChatGPT Wallet on 13 November, built with American Express, Mastercard and Visa, letting its “dots” agents pay for purchases for US ChatGPT Pro subscribers with eligible US-issued Amex, Chase, Mastercard and Visa cards. The agent never sees the card: after the user reviews the items, total and card and approves with Face ID or a fingerprint, the network issues a payment token for that purchase, and each approval creates a record of what the user authorised. Card rewards and dispute protections carry over, OpenAI says it expects users to later pre-approve purchases within spending limits, categories or time periods, and it asked businesses to make sure agents are not inadvertently blocked and that risk systems recognise legitimate agent purchases. Per-purchase biometric approval and an authorisation record answer much of the “whose instruction was it?” question; the harder test for issuers and fraud teams comes when delegated, pre-approved spending arrives, so decide now what limits, signals and liability rules you would accept. OpenAI · CNBC
  • JPMorgan Chase, the largest US card issuer by purchase volume, has been blocking AI agents when customers hand them their card details, while Stripe has embraced agentic purchasing and cards from Bank of America, Capital One and Citigroup are already being used by agents when customers enable them, the Wall Street Journal reported on 13 November. Agent card volumes are still small, but the US Treasury is asking banks about frameworks for AI and payments, and one card-tech executive said banks “can slow this down for a year or two, but they can’t stop” it. Issuers are now picking camps on agent spending; whichever side you choose, decide how you will recognise an agent-initiated card transaction, what limits apply and who carries the fraud and dispute loss. WSJ via Mint
  • BCG benchmarked 26 global and domestic systemically important banks in AI Safety and Governance in Banking 2026, published on 13 November: 73% now permit AI agents in production for select use cases, but none allow broad production use, at 88% of banks agents are 10% or less of the AI portfolio, and only 8% have a comprehensive, standardised monitoring and control framework that explicitly covers agents. 54% have written restrictions or prohibitions on agentic AI into their risk appetite, 46% reported an AI-related issue or incident in the past 12 months, fewer than half keep a centralised, tool-based AI inventory, and none has a human-oversight framework applied consistently across AI systems. Banks with more mature risk management were 2.5 times more likely to be advanced AI adopters. If your agent controls today are mostly prohibitions, use the window while agents are still a small share of the portfolio to build the inventory, monitoring and pre-approved low-risk patterns that let you scale them. BCG

Editor's note · The checkpoint issue

This week the AI story was about checkpoints: who checks the work, when, and whether you can see the check. India signalled that its most sensitive systems should run on Indian models, and the first candidates arrived, one of them reporting how sure it is. Anthropic's strongest public model watches patterns across your usage, while its customers gained their own veto over each prompt. Nubank tests its banking agents on synthetic customers before real ones, and is honest about how unlike real customers they are. Microsoft put limits on free Copilot that no administrator can see. Mistral, Meta, Google DeepMind and NVIDIA each showed a version of the same pattern: plan big, execute cheap, rehearse before you act. Every fact is linked to its source; the analysis is mine.
