Sebi says every AI agent must be traceable. India's cyber agency has just shown why the approval trail matters

Sebi is preparing guidelines on responsible AI in capital markets, and is studying a domestically governed AI model for smaller firms. SBI's chairman called for defined permissions, limits and audit trails for autonomous systems. And CERT-In reported fraud in which attackers changed approval relationships, then let legitimate payment processes pay out.

By Shashwat Kumar · 2026-11-23 · 5 min read · Analysis

Facts are attributed and linked to their sources. The analysis and opinions are the author's own.

What it is

Speaking at a World Federation of Exchanges event in Mumbai on 19 November, Sebi whole-time member KVR Murty said Sebi is "preparing to issue guidelines for responsible usage of AI in the capital markets, including disclosures and accountability of related entities", ANI reported. He framed the problem plainly: "An AI agent is given a goal, not a fixed set of rules, and it can change how it trades as it learns. So how do you approve a strategy that rewrites itself?" He raised the risk of millions of investors using agents built on similar models and data and exiting at once. "We don't have every answer yet, and I doubt any regulator in this room does. But one principle will not change: every agent, however intelligent, must be traceable, and someone must answer for what it does."

The next day Sebi chairman Tuhin Kanta Pandey said Sebi is exploring a domestically governed AI model for the securities market, "to make AI-based regulatory tools secure, affordable and accessible, particularly for smaller regulated entities", Business Standard reported. A committee is examining whether it could be built as digital public infrastructure. He said the work is in its initial stages and will look at guardrails for risks such as hallucinations.

Also on 19 November, SBI chairman C S Setty said that institutions "must clearly understand what an autonomous system is authorised to do: the information it may access, the decisions it may undertake, and the circumstances in which human intervention becomes mandatory", and called for defined permissions, transaction limits, audit trails and human intervention. "If an institution cannot adequately explain why an AI-backed process denied a loan, flagged a transaction, or initiated an action, it faces serious governance challenges," he said.

On 20 November CERT-In issued an advisory on unauthorised transactions at service providers to banking and financial entities, and at technology providers supporting government-linked payments, Reuters reported. Attackers altered business records or approval relationships and then used legitimate payment processes. CERT-In did not say how much money was paid out. It also said it had identified AI-driven frameworks in attacks across sectors, and issued recommendations for government bodies, non-financial payout platforms and technology service providers.

Why it matters

Read together, these describe the same control from three sides. The market regulator wants every agent traced to someone accountable. India's largest bank describes how: permissions, limits, audit trails and a point where a human must step in. And the cyber agency's advisory shows how the control fails without anyone breaking in. Traceability does not help if the approval chain it traces back to has itself been edited.

The attackers did not break the payment process. They edited who it trusted.

The practical step is the one our "This week" strip suggested on 21 November: treat changes to approval hierarchies, beneficiary records and vendor master data as high-risk events, with dual control and alerts, including at payout partners. Agents add a new item to that list. An agent's permissions are an approval relationship too, so a change to an agent's mandate deserves the same scrutiny as a new signatory. Sebi's guidelines will have to say what "traceable" means in practice. I would want it to cover the record of who changed an agent's authority, not only what the agent did.

Pandey's domestic model is aimed at a real gap: smaller brokers and advisers cannot afford their own AI compliance tools. If it goes ahead, Murty's principle applies to it as well. Someone must answer for what a shared model gets wrong. Earlier issues followed Sebi's kill-switch proposal and the RBI's request for proof of AI-attack preparedness; this week the regulators moved from switches to accountability.

What I'd watch

  • When Sebi publishes its draft AI guidelines, and how they define traceability and accountability.
  • Whether CERT-In or the RBI publish the scale of the approval-tampering losses, or name the platforms involved.
  • What Sebi's committee concludes on building a domestic AI model as public infrastructure.
