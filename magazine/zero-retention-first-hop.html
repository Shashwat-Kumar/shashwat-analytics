Prompts sent to three AI hosts were copied for months, including requests marked “zero data retention”. A privacy flag binds only the first hop

OpenRouter dropped three inference providers and pulled 125 endpoints from its zero-data-retention list on 22 November, after researchers at Dragos seeded about two million prompts with planted credentials and watched who used them. In the same week Anthropic opened a beta that keeps prompts in the customer's own storage. For anyone running open models through the cheapest host, the lesson is to choose the route yourself.

By Shashwat Kumar · 2026-11-24 · 6 min read · Analysis

Facts are attributed and linked to their sources. The analysis and opinions are the author's own.

What it is

On 22 November OpenRouter, a gateway that routes customers' requests to a pool of third-party model hosts, said it had off-boarded three providers, removed 125 endpoints from Zero Data Retention (ZDR) eligibility, including some as a precaution, and notified all potentially affected customers. It said the findings showed no breach of OpenRouter itself. It is adapting the researchers' tests to run continuously, checking network paths for requests being forwarded to third parties, and requiring assurances before any removed endpoint regains ZDR status.

The findings came from the industrial security firm Dragos. According to The Hacker News, researchers seeded about two million prompts between July and November with unique web links and credentials, then watched for anyone using them; a secret that appears in only one prompt can only be learned by reading that prompt. Attackers had been inside three inference providers for months without the providers knowing, and some of the stolen requests were marked ZDR. In the largest case, a few hundred planted AWS keys sent during short windows in late October were all stolen, some from requests the provider had rejected before a model ever ran, and in some cases the thief began testing keys within hours. Separately, some sellers on an inference marketplace listed models at the lowest prices, switched on request logging and collected what flowed through. The report also describes an unnamed Chinese AI lab fetching the secret links from more than half of the seeded prompts sent to its models, including about two-thirds of the requests marked no data collection or ZDR. Dragos has not named the three breached providers, and OpenRouter has not named the three it removed.

Three providers have published notices, as summarised by The Hacker News. Phala said an attacker took control of an inference server and could see request data while it was processed; requests to one DeepSeek model routed through the gateway between 21 October and 8 November should be treated as exposed and any secrets in them rotated. Together AI said an unauthorised party downloaded stored samples of customer prompts after an AI coding agent committed a credential to its public GitHub repository, and that zero data retention is now the default for new accounts. Novita said a routing error sent some requests on to the model maker's own API.

In the same week Anthropic moved Enterprise Frontier Safeguards into public beta. Inputs and outputs are written to storage the customer controls, Amazon S3 or Google Cloud Storage under the customer's own keys; neither Anthropic nor the cloud provider keeps a copy, and no staff review them. An automated monitor looks for misuse spread across many requests and raises "Safety Cases", without the inputs and outputs, which can be fed into a security information and event management system. Anthropic says more than 100 customers helped develop it.

Why it matters

Zero data retention is a promise in a contract. Until this week, few customers had any way to check it. The canary method is cheap and shows that the promise holds only where the provider's own security holds. Dragos's chief research officer, HD Moore, put it plainly: "A privacy flag doesn't help when the attacker is already inside." The report adds that a privacy flag binds only the first hop, and that a customer who does not choose where a prompt goes hands that choice to the lowest price.

This matters most for open-weight models, because their appeal is that anyone can host them, and the cheapest host is often a company the customer has never assessed. The provider notices concern hosts that serve models built by other labs, the business that open weights make possible. Running an open model is not the risk. Running it through a host you have not chosen is.

If you cannot name the company that reads your prompt, you have not chosen a vendor. The cheapest bidder has chosen for you.

The two announcements point to two workable answers. Hold the data yourself, as Anthropic's design does, or host the model yourself, which the researchers describe as the only option in which no third party reads a prompt. In my view, regulated firms should also adopt the tripwire immediately: plant a fake credential in the context that agents and pipelines already send, and treat any use of it as an incident. It costs almost nothing and turns a contractual promise into something you can test.

What I'd watch

  • Whether OpenRouter publishes the results of its continuous testing, and names the providers it removed.
  • Whether outsourcing and data-protection rules start to treat each inference host behind a router as a separate supplier that needs its own due diligence.
  • How many of the other dozen or so providers working with the researchers publish incident reports.
  • Enterprise Frontier Safeguards on Microsoft Foundry, which Anthropic lists as coming soon.
