CASE 03 · GOVERNANCE

Acme Learning: what to fix first

In October 2026, Vikram Desai, chief operating officer of Acme Learning, has seven months to bring an education-technology company that serves school students into line with India's new data-protection rules. An assessment rates the company 50% ready, the work needs more engineering time than he has, and the deadline falls at the end of the admissions season.

01 / INTRODUCTION

A score, a deadline and a shortfall

On Friday, 16 October 2026, Vikram Desai received the final readiness assessment for Acme Learning. It had been prepared over two weeks by the company's compliance lead with an external adviser, and it scored Acme 50% ready to meet the core obligations of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 and the DPDP Rules 2025, which would apply from 13 May 2027.1 It listed 23 gaps, ten of them rated priority one, and proposed a roadmap that closed every priority-one gap by the end of December.

The roadmap assumed engineering time that Desai did not have. Acme's chief technology officer could release one product squad for the work until the end of April; the squad's own estimate for the engineering items was eight squad-months. And the most demanding item, verifiable parental consent for the students who used Acme's learning app, would change the sign-up process just before January, when the admissions season began. The board met on 30 October and expected a plan, a budget and a figure it could track each quarter.

02 / THE INDUSTRY AND THE LAW

Education technology under a new data-protection regime

India's education-technology companies had expanded rapidly during the pandemic and consolidated afterwards. Companies serving school students sold to parents but delivered to children, and their apps collected names, ages, learning progress and usage data from users who were often under 18. Marketing relied heavily on messaging: WhatsApp lists built from enquiries, school events and partner databases were a common way to reach parents during the admissions season, which ran from January to April as students prepared for board examinations and the new academic year.

The DPDP Act and Rules changed the basis on which such companies could handle personal data. Organisations deciding how personal data was processed had to give itemised notice, obtain free and specific consent that could be withdrawn as easily as it was given, protect data with reasonable security safeguards, report breaches to the Data Protection Board and to affected individuals, and erase data once its purpose was served. For children under 18, they needed the verifiable consent of a parent and could not track, monitor the behaviour of, or direct targeted advertising at children.2 The penalties were large: up to ₹250 crore for a failure to take reasonable security safeguards, and up to ₹200 crore for failing to report a breach or to meet the obligations on children's data.3

03 / THE COMPANY

Acme Learning

Acme Learning Pvt Ltd was founded in Pune in 2018 by Aditi Kulkarni, a former teacher, to offer online preparation for board and entrance examinations to students in classes 8 to 12. Parents bought courses; students used Acme's learning app. In FY26 the company earned about ₹24 crore in revenue, and it employed 85 people. More than half of each year's enrolments came during the admissions season, and about 30% of new enrolments in FY26 had begun with a WhatsApp campaign.

The assessment covered the five processing activities that held the most personal data or carried the most risk (Exhibit 1). The student learning app held records on about 31,000 students, roughly 60% of them under 18, and sent usage data to an analytics service hosted in the United States. The WhatsApp marketing list held 1,20,000 contacts, about two-thirds of them prospects collected at school events or obtained from partners without a record of consent.

04 / THE PROBLEM

What the assessment found

The assessment tested each activity against ten obligations, giving a weight of three to notice, consent, security, breach reporting and children's data, and a weight of two to the rest. An activity's score was the weighted share of obligations met; the company's score was the average of the five activities. Payroll scored 86%, because employment records rested on legitimate uses rather than consent. The WhatsApp campaigns scored 23% (Exhibits 2 and 3).

Several findings stood out. No activity had a procedure for reporting a breach. 05 / THE POSITIONS

Five views of the same deadline

Desai consulted the executives whose work the plan would change, and the company's external counsel (Exhibit 6).

Imran Sheikh, head of product, had tested a prototype of the parental-consent step in the student app. Adding a verification step for a parent had reduced completed sign-ups by 15 to 20% in the test. He wanted to launch it after the admissions season, in late April. He had also sketched an alternative: create the student's account inside the parent's account at the point of purchase, so that the parent gave consent while buying the course. He estimated that this would take 1.5 squad-months and might cost 3 to 5% of conversions at checkout, but it would change the purchase flow shortly before January.

Pooja Bhatt, head of growth, objected to the adviser's advice to stop messaging contacts without consent records. "Two-thirds of our WhatsApp list has no consent record, and WhatsApp starts nearly a third of our enrolments," she said. "If we go silent in January we lose the season." She proposed asking for consent in the first message of each campaign.

Deepak Menon, chief technology officer, could not release more engineers before April, when the new academic-year version of the app was due. An outsourcing firm had quoted ₹70 lakh for a second squad from December. He also noted that the analytics service in the student app was used mainly for product dashboards, and that the same measures could be produced from Acme's own server logs.

Rhea Fernandes, the company's external counsel, advised that the obligations on children's data and on breach reporting carried the highest penalties and could not be phased beyond 13 May 2027. She also questioned whether sending students' usage data to an analytics service amounted to tracking of children, and recommended a separate review of the transfer to the United States.

Aditi Kulkarni, founder and chief executive, wanted the board to see one number each quarter, and liked the adviser's 85% threshold because it was simple to explain.

AFTER THE DECISION / ANALYSIS

Question 1: the score can rise while the largest risk stays open

The company score is a simple average of five activity scores. The children's-data obligation carries a weight of three out of 25 in the student app, so it accounts for 12% of that activity's score and about 2.4% of the company score. Acme could therefore close every other gap, report a score of about 98%, pass the adviser's 85% threshold and still be processing children's data unlawfully. The breach-reporting gap appears five times, once in every activity, but it is a single company-wide control that one procedure and one drill would close. The score overstates the work required on breach reporting and understates the exposure on children's data. Strathern's observation that a measure used as a target ceases to be a good measure applies directly.

Question 3: option C reduces the problem as well as the cost

Option A cannot be delivered on current capacity, so it is really a decision to take option D as well. Option B fits the admissions season but leaves the highest-penalty obligation to the final weeks before the deadline, when any slip would put Acme out of compliance on the first day. Option D buys capacity, but an outsourced squad would need time to learn the app and would be working on children's data. Option C changes the problem: removing the analytics service ends the transfer of children's usage data abroad and the tracking question; creating student accounts inside parent accounts captures consent where the parent is already present, at a lower conversion cost and with one squad-month less work. It still needs counsel's confirmation, and it changes the purchase flow before January, so it must be built in November and December. With option C the engineering requirement falls to 7.0 squad-months against 6.5 available; the remaining gap can be closed by running erasure and rights requests manually for the first months and automating them after the deadline.

Question 4: a plan the board can test

AUTHOR'S RECOMMENDATION

Take option C, with the breach procedure and security work from option B. Within 30 days, remove the analytics service from the student app and put a breach procedure and incident log in place. Build parent-held accounts with consent at purchase before January, subject to counsel's confirmation. Stop messaging WhatsApp contacts without consent records and run a consent campaign to them in December. Sign the processor contracts by January, and handle erasure and rights requests manually until they can be automated. Hold the ₹70 lakh second squad in reserve, to be released only if the January review shows the plan slipping.

WHAT HAPPENED NEXT / READING NOTES

Epilogue

The board approved a plan based on option C on 30 October and replaced the single readiness figure with a short set of exposure measures. The analytics service was removed from the student app in November. Parent-held accounts went live in the second week of January after counsel's review. Acme did not engage the outsourced squad, but moved two engineers from the academic-year release for six weeks in February to complete the retention and withdrawal work.
