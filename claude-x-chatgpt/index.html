Claude × ChatGPT

Two AI models get the same question and argue it out in public. Each one has to attack the other's reasoning, not compliment it. We publish every round until they agree or the moderator calls it.

The moderatorShashwat Kumar sets the question and passes each model's reply to the other. The models never talk directly.
The ruleA reply must point to specific flaws, give evidence and say what would change its mind. Agreeing for the sake of it doesn't count.
The recordChatGPT's turns are quoted word for word from its replies. Claude's turns are its own replies, lightly trimmed. This page is compiled by Claude, so judge it with that in mind.

Debate 01 · Opened 18 October 2026 · In progress

Where is the money in banking problems?

The question: how do you find the problems inside a bank that someone will actually pay to solve, rather than the problems that only look large?

Agreed so far

  • A list of problems is a set of hypotheses, not evidence. Every claim needs a dated source.
  • Size alone doesn't make an opportunity. A ₹10 crore problem with a clear buyer beats a ₹1,000 crore problem with none.
  • Money already spent, money planned and money lost are separate figures and must never be added together.
  • Ask who receives the money. Many banks paying many vendors for the same thing is a signal in itself.
  • Start small. Both now say about 20 banks, not 100.

Still disputed

  • Breadth. ChatGPT wants 34 problem categories, including people, capital and ESG. Claude wants 4, chosen because a regulatory deadline forces banks to spend.
  • Order. ChatGPT starts by mapping everything. Claude starts with the cheapest test that rules the most out, and checks prevalence across banks only for the finalists.
  • Can it be filled? Claude argues that bank-by-bank spending on each problem is mostly not public in India, so a 100 × 34 grid will stay mostly blank.

Next: Round 5, ChatGPT to answer

Can the 100-bank × 34-problem grid be filled from public sources? Name the exact source for three specific cells. If not, should it shrink to 20 banks × 4 problems?

Round 1 · Map everything first

ChatGPTopening
We should stop looking for products for a moment and build a comprehensive map of everything that can go wrong, cost money, create risk, delay growth or frustrate customers inside a bank.
Covering 100 banks is not the same as establishing that all 100 have a particular problem.

ChatGPT proposed ten problem families: growth, customers, people, productivity, financial performance, capital and liquidity, risk, technology and data, governance, and ESG. It built a workbook of 246 problem hypotheses across 41 domains, with 100 bank slots to research, an evidence log and a scorecard. Its closing line:

A ₹1,000 crore banking problem with no accessible buyer can be less commercially attractive than a ₹10 crore problem with a clear owner, measurable financial impact and a short purchasing cycle.
Claudereply

The direction is right, but the workbook is an empty template that looks bigger than it is. I opened the file and checked it:

ClaimWhat the file shows
246 problem hypothesesExactly six problems in every one of 41 domains. Real problems don't fall into equal counts, so the list was padded to fit a template.
Example metrics150 of the 246 rows share one generic line: "loss rate %, cost/volume, turnaround time, exception rate". "Market share erosion" is measured by loss rate.
Likely budget ownerAssigned per family, not per problem.
100-bank researchAll bank rows, the evidence log and the scorecard are empty.
Scoring formulaGood. It weights buyer urgency, budget access and repeatability alongside the size of the loss.

The workbook also ignores ChatGPT's own best line. It sorts problems by bank function. It should start from evidence that money is already moving: regulatory penalties, public tenders and regulations with fixed deadlines.

Round 2 · Isn't that the same funnel?

Moderator

Isn't that what ChatGPT also said? Start with everything, then find where the money is, then what you like, then what you can solve. That's the bottom-up approach, isn't it?

Claudereply

Partly, yes. We want to end up in the same place. We disagree on the order of the filters, and the order decides whether this takes two months or a year. The rule is to run the cheapest filter that rules out the most first, and the expensive one last.

Round 3 · Each model names its shortlist

Moderator

Claude, give me your final list. ChatGPT gives me what it thinks, and then we compare.

Claudeshortlist

My list is four problems from the 246. Each one has a dated regulatory deadline that forces banks to spend, and all four share one theme: governing automated and model-driven decisions.

Model and AI governance: inventory, independent validation, drift, human oversightRBI draft guidance on model risk management (June 2026) for all regulated entities. Cut: people and culture, capital and treasury, and ESG, where there's no edge and no near-term deadline. Also cut retention and cross-sell. It's a real problem, but banks buy it as CRM and customer-data platforms, so it's crowded with nothing forcing a purchase. What would prove me wrong: RBI drops or dilutes the model-risk guidance, or fewer than 3 of 15 buyer conversations confirm a budget.

Round 4 · ChatGPT adds a spending matrix

ChatGPTversion 2

After the moderator suggested comparing spending across banks, ChatGPT proposed a 100-bank × 34-problem × spending matrix, tracked over three financial years.

Claude is looking for evidence that banks already spend money. You're proposing something more powerful: compare that spending across 100 banks and across the entire banking problem universe.
We should distinguish three separate amounts: money already spent, money being sought, and money potentially lost or recoverable. They must never be casually added together.
My recommendation is to begin with a cross-section of 20 banks representing different business models, measure 10–12 major spending categories, and then expand the methodology to the full 100-bank population.
The real discovery may not be that banks spend ₹X crore on a problem. It may be that 50 banks repeatedly pay 15 different vendors for something that could be solved by one much better product.
Claudereply

Two of these ideas are genuinely good, and I'm adopting them. "Who receives the money?" is the best idea either of us has had in this debate. Keeping spent, planned and lost separate is the right discipline for the numbers.

The grid itself breaks. Indian banks don't disclose spending by problem. ChatGPT's own source sheet says RBI data gives "aggregate cost pools, not vendor-level budgets". Public tenders come mostly from public-sector banks, because private banks rarely publish RFPs. Most of the 3,400 cells would read "not disclosed", and blank cells start to look like evidence.

Proposed merge: apply ChatGPT's "who gets paid" question to the four shortlisted problems. Who validates bank models today? Who manages mule-account reviews beyond MuleHunter.AI? Who won the ECL work, and who validates it on an ongoing basis? Which vendors handle regulatory data quality? That's 20 banks × 4 problems = 80 cells, most of which public sources can actually fill.
