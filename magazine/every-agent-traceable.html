India's market regulator says every AI agent must be traceable and someone must answer for it. Its cyber agency showed why the same week

On 19 November a Sebi whole-time member said guidelines on responsible AI in capital markets, including disclosures and accountability, are being prepared. A day later Sebi's chairman said it is exploring a domestically governed AI model for the market, and CERT-In reported attackers who changed payment approvals and let legitimate processes pay out. Traceability is now the test that matters.

By Shashwat Kumar · 2026-11-24 · 5 min read · Analysis

Facts are attributed and linked to their sources. The analysis and opinions are the author's own.

What it is

Speaking at a World Federation of Exchanges event in Mumbai on 19 November, Sebi whole-time member KVR Murty said the regulator "is also preparing to issue guidelines for responsible usage of AI in the capital markets, including disclosures and accountability of related entities", ETCFO reported. He described the problem directly: "An AI agent is given a goal, not a fixed set of rules, and it can change how it trades as it learns. So how do you approve a strategy that rewrites itself?" He raised the risk of millions of investors using agents built on similar models and data exiting at once, and set out one principle: "every agent, however intelligent, must be traceable, and someone must answer for what it does."

The next day, Sebi chairman Tuhin Kanta Pandey said Sebi is exploring a domestically governed AI model for the securities market, possibly built as digital public infrastructure with market participants, "to make AI-based regulatory tools secure, affordable and accessible, particularly for smaller regulated entities", Business Standard reported. He said the work is at an initial stage and would examine guardrails against risks such as hallucination.

Also on 20 November, India's cyber agency CERT-In said in an advisory that it had reported unauthorised transactions at service providers to banking and financial firms and at technology providers supporting government-linked payments, according to a Reuters report carried by BusinessLine. Attackers altered business records or approval relationships and then used legitimate payment processes. CERT-In did not say how much was paid out. It also said it had identified AI-driven frameworks being used in attacks across sectors, and issued recommendations for government bodies, non-financial payout platforms and technology service providers.

Why it matters

Issue 03 reported Sebi's interest in a kill switch for algorithms. This week's language goes further: accountability attaches to an entity, not to a model, and an agent must be traceable to it. That matches the cover story's US banks and Microsoft's named-manager agents. Murty's question about approving a strategy that rewrites itself has no easy answer, but traceability is the precondition for any answer: if you cannot reconstruct what an agent did and on whose authority, you cannot approve, supervise or unwind it.

The CERT-In advisory shows the same point from the attacker's side. The attackers did not need to break the payment system. They changed the records that tell the system whom to trust, such as who may approve and which account a vendor is paid into, and then let normal processes run. As agents take on more back-office work, they will be reading and sometimes updating exactly those records.

The attackers did not break the payment system. They changed whom it trusted, and let it do its job.

In my view, every Indian bank, NBFC and payout platform should treat changes to vendor bank details and approver mappings as high-risk events in their own right: logged, independently alerted and confirmed through a separate channel, whether the change is made by a person or an agent.

What I'd watch

  • The text of Sebi's AI guidelines: how they define an agent, and whether accountability sits with the broker, the adviser or the technology provider.
  • Whether the domestically governed model is offered to smaller intermediaries as shared infrastructure, and who would be liable for its output.
  • Whether CERT-In or the RBI name the affected service providers or publish the losses.
